The pipeline rebuilds the publication's own headline (150.3 million appointments, 8.2 million DNAs, 5.4%) from the provider-level file to the last appointment, and reproduces NHS England's published DNA proportion for every ethnic group exactly. If either check fails, nothing is reported.

Five things to take from this

  1. Look at hospital cancellations before patient behaviour. Hospitals cancelled 13.9m appointments, 1.7 times the 8.2m that patients missed, and the hospital cancellation rate rose from 6.8% in 2015-16 to 9.3% while the DNA rate fell from 6.6% to 5.4%. London cancels 13.9% of appointments; the South West 5.6%. Part of that gap may be recording practice, which is itself worth checking.
  2. Non-attendance is concentrated, so support can be targeted without blaming anyone. Men aged 20-24 miss 13.8% of booked appointments against 3.5% for people aged 75-79. Black patients' DNA rate is 8.8%, 1.81 times the White rate (4.9%). These are the groups to involve in redesigning reminders, booking and timing, not to penalise.
  3. Do not read the ethnicity gap as a fact about ethnicity yet. In the 2021 Census the median age was 43 for the White group, 32 for the Asian and Black groups and 19 for the Mixed group (England and Wales). DNA rates fall steeply with age, so some of the gap is an age effect that these published tables cannot remove. And 18.9% of attendances carry no ethnicity at all. Better recording is a precondition for monitoring inequality, not an afterthought.
  4. Remote appointments are missed less often, but that is not proof they cause better attendance. First appointments by telephone or video have a recorded DNA rate of 4.3% against 7.0% in person. Patients, specialties and the way a missed call is recorded all differ between the two. A remote-first change should be piloted with an evaluation plan and with in-person routes kept open.
  5. Compare trusts like with like before acting on a league table. On plain statistical limits 144 of 151 trusts look like outliers; allowing for real between-trust variation, 6 sit above and 6 below the 99.8% limits. Allowing for age changes the verdict for 5 trusts, and comparing each trust only with trusts of the same type changes it again. Only 3 stand out in the same direction however they are compared: North Staffordshire Combined Healthcare NHS Trust (higher); Norfolk and Norwich University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust (lower); Cornwall Partnership NHS Foundation Trust (lower). Those are the ones worth a conversation with the trust's information team, starting with how missed appointments are recorded. 22 trusts report no cancellations to HES at all, so rates here leave cancellations out of the denominator.

Ethnicity: a real gap, measured on incomplete data

With 97 million appointments in the White group alone, the 95% intervals are a few hundredths of a percentage point wide, so chance is not the issue. Age structure and missing ethnicity are. Under NHS England's Core20PLUS5 approach, local systems define their own PLUS groups, and ethnic minority communities are among those NHS England expects to see identified. The most deprived areas have the lowest first-attendance rate

The most deprived tenth of areas has 4.9% fewer first attendances per head than the least deprived tenth. Need for care is higher in deprived areas, but their populations are also younger, so a crude rate cannot say whether access is worse. It is a question to test with age- and need-adjusted local data, not a finding.

Comparing trusts like with like

A single league table of trusts mixes acute hospitals with mental health, community and specialist trusts, whose patients and clinics are not alike. This section compares each trust three ways: against England, after allowing for the age of its patients, and against trusts of the same type, as classified in NHS England's Estates Returns Information Collection (ERIC). A trust that stands out on every view is worth a question; one that stands out on only one is mostly telling you about the comparison.

Why cancellations are left out of the rate. 22 of the 151 trusts report no patient or hospital cancellations to HES at all, while at a typical trust cancellations are around a fifth of appointments. Dividing DNAs by every appointment with a recorded outcome, as version 0.2.2 of this analysis did, made those trusts' rates look higher purely because of how they record cancellations. On that older definition 4 trusts sat above the limits and 2 below.

Sensitivity. Fitting the national model on the untransformed proportion scale puts 10 trusts outside the limits rather than 12, and Birmingham Community Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust and Birmingham Women's and Children's NHS Foundation Trust are flagged only on that scale. Treat those two as borderline. The age adjustment uses the 11 age bands HES publishes for each trust. HES rounds trust counts to the nearest 5 and suppresses very small ones, so a trust's age bands do not always add up to its total: expected counts move by at most 0.12%, and observed DNAs by more than 1% at only 2 trusts (Derbyshire Community Health Services NHS Foundation Trust: 10 of 490, Cornwall Partnership NHS Foundation Trust: 20 of 115 DNAs in suppressed cells), none of which changes a verdict.

Trusts are identified by joining HES provider codes to the ODS register of NHS trusts (etr). 14 large trusts appear in HES with a -X suffix that the HES metadata does not explain; they match ODS once the suffix is removed. 235 other providers with 10,000+ appointments are independent-sector organisations (or a closed trust code) and are not on these charts.

In general practice, mode matters even more

In 2026-09, 60% of GP appointments were face to face and 12% video or online. "Online" includes asynchronous online consultations, where there is little to miss, so the near-zero rate is partly a property of the mode itself.

Data quality and reconciliation

  • HES headline rebuilt exactly from the provider file: 150,317,821 appointments, 8,151,086 DNAs.
  • GP September 2026: the publication's headline is 35.1 million appointments "recorded across General Practice"; the National Overview file covers the 34.1 million in GP appointment books from 98.9% of practices. This file sums to 34,134,084. The first version of this project typed in the 35.1m headline next to percentages from a narrower base.
  • Every regional and provider count in the HES provider file is a multiple of 5 and small counts appear as *; national figures are exact. Suppressed cells are counted as zero in provider totals and the number affected is recorded per trust.
  • 18.9% of attendances have ethnicity 'Not known' or 'Not stated'.
  • HES assigns deprivation deciles with the 2010 Index of Multiple Deprivation.
